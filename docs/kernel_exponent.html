The redistribution-kernel exponent, with nothing hidden

For each grid cell, the kernel weight is exp(…) of a single sum. Here is that sum, fully decomposed — every term traced back to where it came from.

Three sources are added together inside the exponent. L is the step length (distance from the animal to the cell) and α the turn angle to reach it. shape, scale and κ are the parameters of h — the Gamma×von Mises cloud the random points were sampled from.

η  the fit βL·L + βlogL·log(L) + βcos·cos(α) + (habitat) the linear predictor returned by the model — adjustments relative to h, plus habitat
phi  h’s baseline (shape − 1)·log(L) − (1/scale)·L + κ·cos(α) the log of the very cloud we sampled from — puts h back in
Jacobian  geometry −1·log(L) converts a length-and-angle density into a per-map-area density (the ring-circumference factor)

Grouped by covariate — so nothing disappears

Collect the terms above by which covariate each one multiplies. This is the step that usually gets skipped, which makes it look like terms vanish or transform into one another. They don’t — each lands in its own row.

log(L) shape − 1 βlogL −1 shape − 2 + βlogL
L −1/scale βL — −1/scale + βL

Concrete, for deer 5004_fa_2017: h is Gamma(shape 1.49, scale 91.2 m) for step length and von Mises(κ 0.48) for angle. So at any cell, phi = 0.49·log(L) − L/91.2 + 0.48·cos(α) — these coefficients are known, not estimated.

The three things that look like sleight of hand

“Why subtract 1 from shape?”

That −1 isn’t a correction — it’s what a Gamma density is. A Gamma with shape a has the form La−1·e−L/scale; take the log and the power on log(L) is literally a−1. There is also a separate −1 on that same row from the Jacobian — two different −1’s, from two different places, that happen to land on the same covariate. That pair is exactly why Klappstein Table 1 recovers the step-length shape with a +2.

“Why L/scale in one but β·log(L) in the other?”

L and log(L) are two different covariates, on two different rows. A Gamma needs both: the log(L) coefficient carries the shape, the L coefficient carries the rate (1/scale). Separate knobs, so they never mix.

“Why did κ·cos(α) become −1?”

It didn’t — it is sitting untouched in the cos(α) row. The −1 belongs only to the log(L) row (the Jacobian). Showing just one row and calling it “phi” is what made the L and cos(α) rows look like they vanished.

Put it back together

Written as a product instead of a sum-of-logs, the cell weight is simply:

h(L,α) · exp(η) / L

… then normalize: add up the weights over every cell and divide each by that total. Now the grid sums to 1 and each cell is a probability.

Connection to the GAM. Nothing in this decomposition changes for a GAM — the only difference is how η is obtained: predict(gam, type="link") in place of a model.matrix %×% coefs multiply, because the effects can be smooth rather than straight lines. The phi baseline (from h) and the −log(L) geometry are identical. This is exactly amt::ssf_weights(…, compensate.movement = TRUE).
